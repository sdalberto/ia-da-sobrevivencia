# Fire: how to light one with simple things

Fire warms, cooks, boils water, lights, dries clothes, keeps animals away and lifts morale. Knowing how to light a fire without a lighter is one of the most valuable skills.

## The fire triangle

Fire needs three things at the same time: **heat + fuel + oxygen**. If one is missing, it goes out. Most failures come from damp material or skipping steps in fuel size.

## The three materials (prepare EVERYTHING before lighting)

1. **Tinder:** catches fire from a spark or ember. Examples: cotton with petroleum jelly, fine steel wool, shredded dry bark (bamboo, eucalyptus), shredded dry grass, paper, sawdust, dryer lint, abandoned bird's nest, char cloth (see below), dried orange peel, corn chips (they burn because of the fat).
2. **Kindling:** from the thickness of a matchstick up to a pencil, very dry. Dead branches still attached to the tree are usually drier than those on the ground. If it breaks with a "snap", it's dry.
3. **Fuel wood:** from the thickness of a thumb up to a wrist, then larger logs. Split the wood to expose the dry interior.

Rule of thumb: gather twice as much kindling as you think you need.

## Ignition methods

### Matches and lighters (prepare them)

- Keep matches in a sealed jar. "Storm-proof" (wind- and waterproof) matches work even in rain.
- A lighter without gas still sparks: use the flint on fine tinder (cotton, steel wool).

### Flint / ferrocerium rod ("ferro rod")

1. Rest the tip of the rod inside the tinder.
2. Scrape the rod with the spine of a knife or the striker, firmly, **pulling the rod back** (instead of pushing the knife forward), so you don't scatter the tinder nest.
3. Sparks at ~3,000 °C easily light cotton, fibers and char cloth.

### Battery + steel wool

- Touch both terminals of a 9 V battery to fine steel wool, or use two AA batteries in series with wires.
- The wool glows instantly. Blow gently inside the tinder nest.
- Caution: keep steel wool away from 9 V batteries (it can ignite on its own in a backpack).

### Lens, bottle or mirror (sunny day)

- Magnifying glass, lens from glasses for farsightedness (positive prescription), binocular or camera lens.
- A PET bottle or plastic bag **filled with water** works as a lens.
- The bottom of a soda can polished with toothpaste or chocolate works as a parabolic mirror.
- Focus the smallest possible point of light on dark material (char cloth, dark paper, dry fungus). Be patient: it can take from 10 seconds to a few minutes.

### Bow drill (friction)

It is the most reliable primitive method, but it requires practice:

- **Fireboard (base):** dry, soft wood (e.g. embaúba *Cecropia* spp., fig, cedar, willow, poplar, dry sisal/agave). Carve a small socket and a "V" notch to its center.
- **Spindle:** a straight, dry stick of the same wood or slightly harder, 20–25 cm long and as thick as a thumb.
- **Bow:** a slightly curved branch the length of your arm, with a cord (shoelace, paracord).
- **Handhold:** a stone with a hollow, hardwood or bone; lubricate with fat or a leaf to reduce friction on top.
- Long, steady strokes until thick smoke comes out and the dark dust accumulated in the notch becomes an **ember**. Transfer the ember to the tinder nest and blow gently.

### Fire piston and others

Fire pistons, hand drills and bamboo fire saws also work, but require more practice or specific equipment.

## How to make char cloth

1. Cut scraps of **100% cotton** (old jeans, T-shirt).
2. Put them in a metal tin with a lid (mint tin, cocoa tin) with **a small hole** in the lid.
3. Put the tin in the fire. Smoke comes out of the hole. When the smoke stops, remove the tin and let it cool **closed**.
4. The cloth should come out black and intact. It catches with a single spark or with a lens.

It also works with dry fungi, corn cob pith and sisal cord.

## Building the fire

- **Tipi (cone):** tinder in the center, kindling leaning around it. Catches quickly, good for starting.
- **Log cabin:** a square of layered wood around the tipi. Burns steadily, good for cooking.
- **Star:** thick logs pointing to the center; push them in as they burn. Saves wood all night.
- **Reflector fire:** a wall of stones or logs behind the fire sends heat back to the shelter.
- **Dakota fire hole (double hole):** one hole for the fire and a tunnel for air. Almost smokeless, protected from wind, efficient for cooking.

## Lighting in rain or cold

- Look for dry material **inside** dead logs, under dense trees and under bark.
- Make a platform of branches so the fire isn't on wet ground.
- Carve feather sticks: thin curls still attached to a stick, which expose dry wood.
- Pine resin (resinous pine, "fatwood") burns even when damp.
- Cotton with petroleum jelly and inner-tube rubber are great improvised accelerants.

## Safety

- Clear a 3 m circle around it, free of dry leaves. Keep water or soil nearby.
- Never make a fire under low branches, on peat or roots (fire can spread underground).
- **Carbon monoxide:** never use a brazier, camp stove, generator or barbecue indoors. The gas has no smell and kills people in their sleep. Symptoms: headache, dizziness, nausea, drowsiness. Ventilate and get outside.
- Put it out completely: water, stir, more water, until you can touch the ashes with the back of your hand.
