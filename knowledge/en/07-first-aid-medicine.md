# First aid and health care

> ⚠️ **MEDICAL WARNING:** these are first aid instructions based on recognized public guidelines, cited in each section. They **do not replace** doctors, nurses or emergency services. Whenever possible, seek care (in Brazil: SAMU 192 / Fire Department 193; 112 in many countries; 911 in the US/Canada). For medicines, follow the package insert and the prescription. Take an in-person first aid course: practice saves lives.

## Initial assessment: safety and X-ABCDE

1. **Is the scene safe?** Don't become a second victim (fire, electricity, traffic, collapse).
2. **Responsive?** Tap the shoulders and call out loudly.
3. **X — eXsanguinating hemorrhage:** severe bleeding? Control it first (see below).
4. **A — airway:** open? Tilt the head and lift the chin (if there is no suspected neck injury).
5. **B — breathing:** look, listen and feel for up to 10 seconds. No breathing or only "gasps" (gasping) = cardiac arrest → CPR.
6. **C — circulation:** pallor, cold and clammy skin, rapid pulse = shock.
7. **D — level of consciousness (disability).**
8. **E — exposure:** look for other injuries and protect from the cold.

**Recovery position:** an unconscious person who is breathing normally, with no suspected spinal injury, should be placed on their side so they don't choke on vomit.

> Source: International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies (IFRC) — *International First Aid, Resuscitation and Education Guidelines 2020*.

## Cardiac arrest: CPR (cardiopulmonary resuscitation)

**Adult who is unresponsive and not breathing normally:**

1. Call for help and an AED (defibrillator) if one exists.
2. Hands stacked on the **center of the chest** (lower half of the breastbone), arms straight.
3. Compress **5 to 6 cm** deep, **100 to 120 times per minute** (the rhythm of the song "Stayin' Alive"), letting the chest come back up fully between compressions.
4. If trained: 30 compressions and 2 rescue breaths. If not: **continuous compressions only**.
5. Don't stop until the person starts breathing again, help arrives or you are exhausted. Take turns every 2 minutes if there are more people.

**Child (1 year to puberty):** one or two hands, depth of about 1/3 of the chest (~5 cm). **Infant (<1 year):** two fingers, about 4 cm. In children and drowning victims, rescue breaths are especially important.

> Source: American Heart Association — *2020 Guidelines for CPR and Emergency Cardiovascular Care* (and updates). https://cpr.heart.org

## Choking (airway obstruction)

- **Coughing forcefully?** Encourage them to cough; don't hit them.
- **Can't cough, speak or breathe (adult and child > 1 year):** give **5 firm blows between the shoulder blades** with the person leaning forward, then **5 abdominal thrusts** (Heimlich maneuver: closed fist above the navel, pulling inward and upward). Alternate until the airway clears.
- **Pregnant women and people with obesity:** chest thrusts instead of abdominal thrusts.
- **Infant (<1 year):** face down along your forearm, head lower, **5 back blows**; turn over and give **5 chest thrusts** with two fingers. **Do not give abdominal thrusts to infants.**
- If they become unconscious: start CPR; look in the mouth before rescue breaths and remove the object only if visible.

> Source: IFRC 2020; European Resuscitation Council Guidelines 2021 — First Aid / Basic Life Support.

## Severe bleeding

1. **Firm direct pressure** on the wound with a clean cloth, gauze or your own hand (with a glove or plastic) for at least 10 minutes, without letting up to "take a look".
2. If it soaks through, **don't remove it**: put more cloth on top and keep pressing.
3. Deep wound (armpit, groin, neck): **pack the wound** with clean gauze/cloth, pushing it in, and keep strong pressure.
4. **Tourniquet** — for life-threatening bleeding in an arm or leg that does not stop with pressure:
   - Place it **5–7 cm above the wound** (never over a joint); if you don't know where the wound is, place it "high and tight" at the top of the limb.
   - Tighten until the bleeding **stops**. It hurts — that is normal.
   - **Write down the time** (on the forehead or on the tourniquet). Do not loosen it; get the person to medical care.
   - Improvised: a wide strip (3–5 cm) + a stick to twist (windlass). Thin cord and wire cut the skin and don't work well.
5. Lay the person down and keep them warm (shock prevention).

> Source: American College of Surgeons — *Stop the Bleed*. https://www.stopthebleed.org ; IFRC 2020.

## Shock

Pale, cold and clammy skin, rapid and weak pulse, rapid breathing, confusion, thirst.

- Control the cause (bleeding, dehydration, allergy).
- Lay the person down; if there is no leg/spinal injury, you may raise the legs about 30 cm.
- Keep them warm with blankets, insulate from the ground.
- Seek urgent care.

> Source: IFRC 2020.

## Burns

1. Move away from the heat source. If clothing catches fire: **stop, drop and roll**.
2. **Cool with cool (not ice-cold) running water for at least 20 minutes** — this still helps up to 3 hours after the burn.
3. Remove rings, watches and clothing that isn't stuck before swelling sets in.
4. Cover with **kitchen plastic wrap** (in layers, not wrapped tightly) or a clean, damp cloth.
5. **Do not use** ice, butter, toothpaste, coffee grounds, oil. Do not pop blisters.
6. Prevent hypothermia: cool the burn, but keep the rest of the body warm.
7. Offer fluids if the person is conscious.

**Seek medical care** if: larger than the victim's palm, deep (white, brown, painless), on the face, hands, feet, genitals, joints, electrical or chemical burn, smoke inhalation (hoarseness, soot in the nose), or in children and elderly people.

**Chemical:** remove contaminated clothing and rinse with plenty of running water for 20 minutes or more. Dry powder: brush off before rinsing.

> Source: IFRC 2020; British Burns Association — First Aid Clinical Practice Guidelines (2018); Sociedade Brasileira de Queimaduras (Brazilian Burns Society).

## Wounds and infection prevention

1. Wash your hands. Control the bleeding.
2. **Irrigate the wound with plenty of drinking water** (boiled and cooled, or treated): use a syringe or a plastic bag with a hole to make a jet. Washing well is more important than antiseptic.
3. Remove visible dirt with clean (boiled) tweezers.
4. Clean, recent (< 6–8 hours) and superficial wounds can be closed with adhesive strips ("steri-strip" type or tape cut into a butterfly shape).
5. **Do not close** dirty wounds, puncture wounds, animal/human bites or old wounds: clean them, cover with a clean moist dressing and change it daily.
6. Tetanus: keep your vaccination up to date **before** the crisis (booster every 10 years).

**Signs of infection:** spreading redness, warmth, swelling, increasing pain, pus, bad smell, red streaks running up the limb, fever. A spreading infection is a medical emergency — antibiotics only with professional guidance.

> Source: Werner, D. — *Onde Não Há Médico* (*Where There Is No Doctor*) (Hesperian Health Guides), chapter on wounds. https://hesperian.org ; WHO — *Best Practice Guidelines on Emergency Surgical Care in Disaster Situations*.

## Fractures, sprains and dislocations

- Suspect a fracture if there is severe pain, deformity, swelling, inability to move or bear weight.
- **Immobilize** in the position found, including the joint above and below the injury, with an improvised splint (sticks, cardboard, rolled-up magazines) and strips of cloth. Pad it.
- Check circulation below the splint (color, temperature, sensation) and loosen it if it turns purple or numb.
- **Open fracture:** control the bleeding without pressing on the bone, cover with a clean damp cloth, do not push the bone back in; high risk of infection — seek medical care.
- **Suspected spinal injury** (fall from height, diving, accident): don't move the person unless necessary; keep head and neck aligned.
- Sprain: rest, ice wrapped in cloth (20 min every 2–3 h), light compression and elevation.

> Source: IFRC 2020; Werner, D. — *Onde Não Há Médico* (*Where There Is No Doctor*).

## Diarrhea and dehydration — oral rehydration solution

Diarrhea kills mainly through dehydration, especially in children. **The main measure is to replace fluids.**

**Homemade solution (when oral rehydration salts – ORS – from a pharmacy/clinic are not available):**

- **1 liter of drinking water** (boiled and cooled, or treated)
- **6 level teaspoons of sugar** (~25–30 g)
- **½ level teaspoon of salt** (~2.5 g)

Mix well. It should taste no saltier than tears. Offer small, frequent sips after each bowel movement. Make a fresh batch every 24 hours.

- Keep feeding (and breastfeeding babies).
- **Children:** the WHO recommends **zinc** for 10–14 days along with ORS (dose according to age and package insert).
- Signs of severe dehydration: very drowsy, sunken eyes, skin that is slow to go back when pinched, unable to drink, no urine. **Seek urgent care.**
- Diarrhea with blood, high fever or lasting more than 2 weeks also needs evaluation.

> Sources: WHO/UNICEF — *Oral Rehydration Salts: production of the new ORS* (2006) and *Clinical management of acute diarrhoea* (2004); Werner, D. — *Onde Não Há Médico* (*Where There Is No Doctor*), "rehydration drink". Ministério da Saúde (Brazilian Ministry of Health) — management of acute diarrhea.

## Fever

- Light clothing, a cool environment, plenty of fluids.
- Fever reducers (paracetamol/acetaminophen, dipyrone/metamizole, ibuprofen) **only according to the package insert** and body weight, especially in children. Do not give aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid) to children and teenagers with fever (Reye's syndrome) or when dengue is suspected.
- **Warning signs (emergency):** stiff neck, skin spots that don't fade when pressed with a drinking glass, confusion or excessive drowsiness, seizure, difficulty breathing, fever in a baby under 3 months, signs of severe dengue (intense abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, bleeding).

> Source: Ministério da Saúde (Brazilian Ministry of Health) — Dengue: diagnóstico e manejo clínico (Dengue: diagnosis and clinical management); WHO — Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI).

## Hypothermia (cold)

Shivering, slurred speech, clumsiness, confusion; in severe cases the shivering **stops**.

1. Move the person to a sheltered place, **remove wet clothing** and dry them.
2. Insulate from the ground and wrap in blankets, a sleeping bag and a waterproof layer (plastic, emergency blanket).
3. Warm the **torso** (armpits, chest, groin) with warm water bottles wrapped in cloth; skin-to-skin contact helps.
4. If conscious and able to swallow: warm, sweet drinks. **No alcohol.**
5. In severe hypothermia, **move the person very gently** and keep them horizontal (risk of arrhythmia).

> Source: Wilderness Medical Society — *Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Out-of-Hospital Evaluation and Treatment of Accidental Hypothermia: 2019 Update*.

## Heatstroke and heat exhaustion

- **Heat exhaustion:** heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, nausea, cramps. Shade, lie down, loosen clothing, drink water or rehydration solution.
- **Heatstroke — EMERGENCY:** very high temperature, confusion, strange behavior, seizure, hot skin (dry or sweaty).
  - **Cool immediately:** the best option is **immersion in cold water** up to the neck; failing that, continuously wet the whole body and fan, and apply cold, wet towels over the whole body.
  - Do not give fluids if the person is confused or unconscious.

> Source: Wilderness Medical Society — *Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Heat Illness: 2019 Update*; IFRC 2020.

## Snakebite (Brazil)

**Do:**
- Move away from the snake; don't try to catch it (a photo from a distance helps with identification).
- Keep the victim **calm, lying down and with the limb still**.
- Remove rings, bracelets and shoes (the limb may swell).
- Wash the area with soap and water.
- Take them urgently to a referral hospital: **antivenom is the only effective treatment**.
- Offer water if conscious.

**DO NOT:** apply a tourniquet or tight band, cut, suck, burn, apply herbs, kerosene, alcohol, coffee grounds or any substance to the bite, or give alcoholic drinks.

**Scorpion:** warm compress and painkiller for pain; **children under 7 and elderly people** can have severe reactions (repeated vomiting, heavy sweating, agitation, shortness of breath) — urgent care.

**Brown recluse spider (Loxosceles):** the lesion worsens over hours to days; seek evaluation.

> Source: Ministério da Saúde (Brazilian Ministry of Health) — Acidentes por animais peçonhentos: o que fazer e como evitar (Venomous animal accidents: what to do and how to avoid them). https://www.gov.br/saude/pt-br/assuntos/saude-de-a-a-z/a/animais-peconhentos

## Severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis)

Swelling of the lips, tongue or throat, shortness of breath, wheezing, widespread hives, dizziness or fainting after an insect sting, food or medicine.

- Use an **epinephrine (adrenaline) auto-injector** (if the person has one) in the outer thigh, even through clothing. It can be repeated in 5–15 min if there is no improvement and a second dose is available.
- Lay the person down with legs raised (or sitting if shortness of breath is worse).
- Antihistamine tablets help with itching, but **do not replace** epinephrine.

> Source: World Allergy Organization — *Anaphylaxis Guidance 2020*; IFRC 2020.

## Emergency childbirth

Birth almost always happens on its own; the helper's role is to **keep things clean, keep them warm and not get in the way**.

1. Wash your hands well; prepare clean cloths, a blanket, boiled thread/cord and a clean (boiled) blade.
2. Let the mother take whatever position feels best. **Do not pull** the baby; just support the head and body as they come out.
3. Dry the baby immediately, place them **skin to skin** on the mother and cover both. If the baby doesn't breathe, rub the back; if still not breathing, give gentle rescue breaths.
4. Wait **1 to 3 minutes** (or until the cord stops pulsing) before tying the cord in two places (~2 and ~5 cm from the navel) and cutting between them with a clean blade.
5. The placenta usually comes out within 30 minutes. **Do not pull** on the cord.
6. Putting the baby to the breast soon helps the uterus contract. Heavy bleeding after the placenta: massage the uterus firmly through the belly (below the navel) and seek urgent help.

> Source: WHO — *Pregnancy, Childbirth, Postpartum and Newborn Care: a guide for essential practice* (3rd ed., 2015); Hesperian — *Where Women Have No Doctor*.

## Long-term health kit

- **Continuous-use medicines:** a stock of medicines for people with chronic conditions (blood pressure, diabetes, thyroid, epilepsy, asthma) — talk to the doctor before the crisis about prescriptions for stockpiling.
- **Basics:** painkillers/fever reducers (paracetamol/acetaminophen, dipyrone/metamizole, ibuprofen), antihistamine, oral rehydration salts, children's zinc, antacid, diaper rash ointment.
- **Dressings:** gauze, compresses, crepe bandages, adhesive/micropore tape, wound-closure strips, adhesive bandages, disposable gloves, saline solution, chlorhexidine or povidone-iodine, scissors, tweezers, thermometer, commercial tourniquet (CAT type), compression bandage, emergency blanket.
- **Prevention:** sunscreen, insect repellent, mosquito nets, toothbrush and dental floss, pads/menstrual cup, condoms.
- **Recommended printed books:** *Onde Não Há Médico* (*Where There Is No Doctor*), *Onde Não Há Dentista* (*Where There Is No Dentist*) and *Where Women Have No Doctor* (Hesperian — free PDFs at hesperian.org).

Check expiry dates every 6 months. Expired medicines lose effectiveness; expired tetracyclines can be toxic — do not use them.
