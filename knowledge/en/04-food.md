# Food: stockpiling, preparation and preservation

## How much a person needs

- Adult with moderate activity: about **2,000 to 2,500 kcal per day**; heavy physical work or cold: 3,000 kcal or more.
- Children, pregnant women and elderly people have different needs. Prioritize protein, fat and vitamins, not just calories.
- A person can survive weeks without food, but quickly loses strength, concentration and immunity. Eating a little and regularly is better than fasting.

## Stockpile for 1 year (estimate per adult)

| Group | Approximate amount per year |
|---|---|
| Grains (rice, wheat, corn, oats, pasta, cassava flour) | 150–180 kg |
| Legumes (beans, lentils, chickpeas, dried peas) | 30–40 kg |
| Oils and fats (vegetable oil, lard, olive oil) | 10–12 L |
| Sugar, honey, rapadura (unrefined cane sugar) | 25–30 kg |
| Salt (iodized) | 3–5 kg (more if you will preserve meat) |
| Powdered milk | 7–15 kg |
| Yeast, baking soda, vinegar | as needed |
| Vitamins / multivitamin | 365 doses |

Golden rules: **store what you eat and eat what you store** (rotation: first in, first out); store in a dry, dark, cool place; use sealed jars and buckets that keep out rodents and insects.

> Source: estimates based on long-term storage guides such as Utah State University Extension — Food Storage. https://extension.usu.edu/preparedness/

### Approximate shelf life (under ideal conditions, sealed packaging, oxygen-free)

- White rice, whole wheat grain, sugar, salt, honey: **many years** (honey and salt practically never spoil).
- Dried beans: years, but they harden over time (soak longer, use baking soda).
- Flours and brown rice: 6–12 months (the fat goes rancid). Whole grains last longer than flour: have a hand mill.
- Oils: 1–2 years unopened.
- Canned goods: 2–5 years. **Discard cans that are bulging, leaking, deeply rusted or that spurt when opened** (risk of botulism).

## Order of consumption in a crisis

1. Food from the fridge (after 2–4 hours without power it starts warming up; keep the door closed).
2. Freezer (full and closed it holds for about 48 hours; half full, about 24 hours).
3. Fresh fruit and vegetables.
4. Dry stock and canned goods.

> Source: USDA — Food Safety During Power Outage. https://www.foodsafety.gov/food-safety-charts/food-safety-during-power-outage

## Cooking while saving fuel

- **Soak grains and beans** (beans 8–12 h) — cuts cooking time in half.
- **Pressure cooker:** saves up to 70% of fuel.
- **Insulated box / "hay box cooker":** bring the food to a boil for a few minutes and place the covered pot inside a box insulated with blankets, styrofoam or hay. It finishes cooking on its own in 2–4 hours.
- **Rocket stove:** made from cans or bricks in an "L" shape; burns thin branches with high efficiency and little smoke.
- **Solar oven:** a box with a black interior, glass lid and aluminum-foil reflectors reaches 120–150 °C on a sunny day.

## Basic recipes without a bakery

### Unleavened bread (flatbread / chapati)

- 2 cups of flour, 1 pinch of salt, about 3/4 cup of water, 1 spoon of oil (optional).
- Knead for 5 minutes, rest for 15, divide into small balls, roll thin and cook in a frying pan or on a hot stone, 1–2 minutes per side.

### Sourdough starter (levain)

1. Mix equal parts whole-wheat flour and lukewarm chlorine-free water (e.g. 50 g + 50 g) in a jar.
2. Cover with a cloth. Every 24 h discard half and feed with another 50 g + 50 g.
3. In 5–10 days it should double in size within 4–8 h of feeding and have a pleasant sour smell. It is ready.
4. Keep feeding it; always save a portion — it lasts indefinitely.

### Sprouts (fresh vitamins without a garden)

Mung beans, lentils, alfalfa, wheat: soak for 8–12 h, drain and rinse 2 times a day in a jar with a mesh lid. Ready in 2–5 days. A great source of vitamin C when there are no greens. Use seeds intended for eating and treated water.

## Preserving food without a refrigerator

### Drying / dehydration

- Fruit, vegetables, herbs and meat in thin slices, in the sun (covered with mesh against flies) or near the fire, until dry and brittle (vegetables) or pliable with no moisture (fruit).
- Store in closed jars.

### Salting (carne de sol, charque — Brazilian salted dried beef)

- Lean meat in thin sheets, covered with coarse salt, stacked for 12–24 h, then exposed to sun and wind for several days.
- Before eating, soak it, changing the water to remove the salt, and cook it well.

### Smoking

- Cold smoke (below 30 °C) for days, or hot smoke (60–80 °C) for hours, always after salting. Use non-resinous woods (avoid pine and eucalyptus).

### Lactic fermentation (sauerkraut, pickles)

- Sliced cabbage + **2% salt by weight** (20 g of salt per kg). Massage until liquid is released, press into a jar so it stays submerged, cover loosely.
- Ferment 1–4 weeks at room temperature. The flavor becomes sour. Mold on the surface: remove it; if it smells rotten or has an odd color inside, discard it.

### Preserving in fat and sugar

- Cooked meat stored covered in lard (confit) lasts weeks in a cool place.
- Sweets and jams with plenty of sugar and cooked fruit last months.

### Home canning in glass jars — beware of botulism

- Fruit and acidic pickles (with vinegar) can be preserved in a boiling-water bath.
- **Low-acid foods (meat, beans, corn, heart of palm, vegetables) require sterilization in a pressure canner** made for canning. A water bath does not kill *Clostridium botulinum* spores.
- If in doubt about a low-acid home-canned food: boil it for 10 minutes before eating, or discard it.

> Sources: USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning (2015); National Center for Home Food Preservation. https://nchfp.uga.edu

## Cassava (manioc): a key food, with care

- **Sweet cassava (aipim, macaxeira in Brazil):** peel and cook well.
- **Bitter cassava:** contains compounds that release **cyanide**. Never eat it raw. Traditional processing: peel, grate, press (discarding the liquid, called manipueira), toast (into flour) or ferment and cook. Tucupi (the yellow cassava broth) must be boiled for hours.
- If you don't know which one it is, treat it as bitter.

> Source: Embrapa Mandioca e Fruticultura (Brazilian Agricultural Research Corporation, cassava and fruit unit); FAO — Roots, tubers, plantains and bananas in human nutrition.
