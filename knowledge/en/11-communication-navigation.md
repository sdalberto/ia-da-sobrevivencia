# Communication, signals and navigation

## Radio: the internet of the blackout

- **AM/FM/shortwave radio** (battery, dynamo or solar): receives official Civil Defense alerts and news from far away. Shortwave reaches other countries at night.
- **License-free radios (no exam):**
  - **CB / Citizens Band (27 MHz, 40 channels; called "PX" in Brazil):** in Brazil it requires a simple registration with Anatel (the national telecom regulator); range of kilometers, much more with an external antenna. **Channel 9** is traditionally reserved for emergencies.
  - **FRS/PMR walkie-talkies:** short range (hundreds of meters to a few km). PMR446 (Europe), FRS/GMRS (US). In Brazil, check for Anatel certification.
- **Amateur (ham) radio (VHF/UHF/HF):** requires a license (in Brazil, an Anatel exam; elsewhere, the national telecom regulator's exam). Amateur radio networks often operate in disasters — it is the best independent communication network. It's worth getting your license before the crisis.
- **Mesh networks** (e.g. Meshtastic with cheap LoRa radios): text messages between phones without internet, with a range of kilometers and hops between devices.

**Family protocol:** agree on fixed listening times (e.g. 5 minutes at the top of every hour, or at 8 a.m., 12 p.m. and 6 p.m.), a main channel and a backup, and a physical meeting point with written notes.

## Distress signals

- **Three of anything** = distress: three fires in a triangle, three whistle blasts, three shots, three flashes.
- **SOS** in Morse code: `··· — — — ···` (3 short, 3 long, 3 short), with light or sound.
- **Signal mirror:** visible from tens of kilometers on a sunny day. Make a "V" with your extended fingers pointing at the target and move the reflection inside the V.
- **Ground signals** for aircraft (large, 3 m or more, contrasting): **V** = I need help; **X** = I need medical assistance; **arrow** = we are going in this direction.
- **Smoke:** by day, dark smoke (rubber, oil) against a light background; or white smoke (green leaves) against a dark forest background.
- A whistle can be heard much farther than a voice and uses less energy.

> Source: US Army ATP 3-50.21 *Survival* (2018), chapter on signaling; Convention on International Civil Aviation (ICAO), Annex 12 — ground-to-air visual signals.

## Navigation without GPS

### By the Sun

- The Sun rises in the **east** and sets in the **west** (exactly only at the equinoxes; it varies throughout the year).
- In the **Southern Hemisphere**, at noon the Sun is to the **north** (outside the tropics). In the Northern Hemisphere, to the south.
- **Shadow-stick method:** push a stick into the ground and mark the tip of the shadow with a stone. Wait 15–30 minutes and mark it again. The line from the first to the second mark points approximately from **west to east**.

### By the stars

- **Southern Hemisphere — Southern Cross:** extend the long axis of the cross (from top to foot) about **4.5 times** its length. That point in the sky is over the **south**. Drop a vertical line down to the horizon.
- **Northern Hemisphere — North Star:** follow the two "edge" stars of the Big Dipper (Ursa Major); Polaris lies about 5 times that distance away and marks **north**.

### Improvised compass

Rub a sewing needle **always in the same direction** on a magnet (or on silk/hair, weaker) about 30–50 times. Place it on a leaf or cork floating on still water. It aligns on the north–south axis (confirm which end is north using the Sun).

### Maps

- Keep **printed maps** of your region (in Brazil, IBGE maps; topographic charts, road maps).
- On your phone, download offline maps (OsmAnd, Organic Maps) **before** the crisis; the phone's GPS works without internet.
- When traveling, note direction and walking time; leave notes with destination and date.

## Reliable information

Rumors spread in crises. Trust official sources (Civil Defense, local radio stations) and confirm information with more than one source before acting.
